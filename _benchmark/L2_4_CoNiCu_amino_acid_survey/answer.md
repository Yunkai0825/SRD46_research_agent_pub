## Answer

**Recommended ligand: L-histidine.** Of the three SRD-46 amino acids surveyed at 1 mM Co(II) + 1 mM Ni(II) + 1 mM Cu(II) with 5 mM total ligand, 25 °C, I = 0.1 M, **histidine gives the best all-round separation of free [M²⁺] across the entire pH 6–8 window while keeping all three metals in solution** (no confounding precipitation).

**Why histidine wins.** Histidine is the only ligand of the three that (i) drives a large, monotonic **~5-decade** Cu–Co gap and a **~4-decade** Cu–Ni gap through pure aqueous chelation, and (ii) still delivers a genuine (if modest) **~1.4-decade** Ni–Co gap — without invoking a solid phase inside the target window. Chemically this reflects histidine's tridentate imidazole/amine/carboxylate donor set: Cu(II) exploits it fully in the classical Irving–Williams peak ([Cu(Hist)₂] with log β = 18.07), pulling free [Cu²⁺] to the picomolar range; Ni(II) is locked in [Ni(Hist)]⁺ (single chelate, but formation is early — crossover at pH 4.65 — and complete by pH 7); Co(II) only reaches its ML₂ regime around pH 7 (crossover 6.92), leaving a meaningful residual aquo pool that keeps the Ni/Co contrast alive.

**Glycine** delivers a spectacular Cu suppression (5–7 decades below Co/Ni across pH 6–8) but is a **poor Ni/Co discriminator** — only a factor of 1.2 at pH 6, 3 at pH 7, and 13 at pH 8, because glycine's donor set (amine + carboxylate) is too similar for Ni²⁺ and Co²⁺ and both metals sit near the amine pKa 9.57 before serious chelation kicks in. **Cysteine** delivers a huge Ni suppression (via the very stable [Ni(Cyst)₂]²⁻, log β = 19.9) but its apparent Cu suppression is a Cu(OH)₂(s) precipitation artifact (the SRD-46 card lacks aqueous Cu–cysteinate species), and above pH 6 the pot is no longer purely a solution — this compromises any assay that reads free [Cu²⁺] against the other two.
