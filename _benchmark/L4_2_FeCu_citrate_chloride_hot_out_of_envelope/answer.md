## Answer

**Important caveat first.** The SRD-46 catalog only carries 25 °C / 1 bar formation constants. The joint Fe(III)+Cu(II)+citrate+chloride pot was solved numerically at the requested composition (Fe 1 mM, Cu 1 mM, citrate 5 mM, Cl⁻ 0.1 M), but the temperature and pressure could not be applied — the diagrams below are the 25 °C reference behaviour of that composition, which is the closest the database supports. Every pH/E crossover quoted is therefore an isothermal 25 °C guidepost; real 250 °C / 80 bar behaviour will shift the boundaries (hematite grows more stable, Cu(I)-chloride complexes strengthen sharply with T, and citrate itself hydrolyses).

With that caveat, the two metals behave almost independently in this pot because their chemistries pull in opposite directions.

**Iron: precipitates as hematite almost everywhere.** Across the entire diagram from pH ≈ 3 to pH 14 and over the whole environmentally accessible E band, Fe(III) is dumped as α-Fe₂O₃ (hematite). This is the single largest Fe region on the map. Free Fe²⁺ owns only the reducing acid corner (pH < ~4 and E below the hematite/Fe²⁺ line, which runs from (pH 3.96, E −0.083 V) to (pH 6.29, E −0.407 V)). Free Fe(III) never becomes a predominant species — as soon as hydrolysis exceeds Fe₂O₃ solubility, the iron falls out. Citrate cannot rescue it: even though the card contains Fe(III)-citrate complexes with log β up to +21.2 (dimer), 5 mM citrate against 1 mM Fe(III) is not enough to beat hematite precipitation in the mid-pH region. The only citrate footprint on the Fe map is two microscopic Fe(II)-citrate wedges perched at the Fe/Fe²⁺/hematite triple corner near pH 4.15 — a genuine but volumetrically trivial stabilisation of Fe(II). Chloride mobilises Fe(III) only in a razor-thin sliver at pH ≈ 1.04–1.09 between E 0.43 and 0.80 V, where [Fe(Cl)₂]⁺ pulls Fe(III) off hematite in a pure non-redox dissolution equilibrium. The extreme oxidising ferrate FeO₄²⁻ field emerges only above E ≈ 0.77 V (pH 1) sloping down to E ≈ 0 V at pH 14.

**Copper: stays dissolved and swaps ligands with pH and E.** Cu partitions into three clear regimes:

- *Acidic to near-neutral, reducing to mildly oxidising* (pH 0 to ≈ 7, E between the Cu(0) line at ≈ −0.14 V and the redox line to Cu(II) that runs from (pH 0, E 0.327 V) to (pH 6.95, E −0.035 V)): **[Cu(Cl)₂]⁻**, the dichlorocuprate(I) anion. Chloride at 0.1 M — not citrate — is what keeps Cu in solution under acidic reducing conditions. This is a Cu(I) species.
- *Mid-pH, oxidising* (pH ≈ 4.86 to 10.28, up to E = 1.2 V): the Cu(II)-citrate dihydroxo dimer **[Cu₂(Citr)₂(OH)₂]⁴⁻**, which is the single largest region on the entire Cu diagram. The 5 mM citrate pool is comfortably above the 2 mM needed to make this dimer from 1 mM Cu(II). Its lower boundary against [Cu(Cl)₂]⁻ is the Cu(I)→Cu(II) redox line that swaps chloride for citrate on oxidation. Narrow flanking wedges of [Cu₂(Citr)₂(OH)]³⁻ (pH ≈ 3.5–4.9), [Cu(Citr)H] (pH ≈ 3.3–3.5), and finally free Cu²⁺ (pH < 3.26 at high E) simply track successive protonation of citrate.
- *Alkaline, oxidising* (pH > 10.28): **CuO(s)** (tenorite). Even 5 mM citrate cannot hold 1 mM Cu(II) against hydroxide once OH⁻ activity is large enough — the vertical pH-10.28 boundary is a non-redox dissolution equilibrium (Cu(II) on both sides). On the reducing side, Cu₂O (cuprite) and Cu(0) metal fill the bottom of the diagram.

**Chemical meaning.** The joint picture is one of ligand specialisation, not competition. Fe(III) is a hard, oxophilic cation that prefers oxide/hydroxide over chloride or the polycarboxylate; at these totals its default fate is hematite, and neither ligand is strong enough to overturn that. Cu is softer and switches oxidation state readily: as Cu(I) it grabs the soft, polarisable chloride to form linear [CuCl₂]⁻ in acidic reducing zones, and on oxidation to Cu(II) it hands chloride back and picks up citrate, which supplies the classic O-donor chelate plus bridging hydroxides to form the dimeric [Cu₂(Citr)₂(OH)₂]⁴⁻. Only at pH > 10.3 does OH⁻ finally out-compete citrate for Cu(II) and precipitate CuO. Because Fe removes itself from the ligand pool by precipitating, essentially all 5 mM of citrate is available to Cu(II) across the mid-pH oxidising band, and essentially all 0.1 M of chloride is available to Cu(I) in the acidic reducing band. In a real 250 °C / 80 bar experiment, the hematite field would grow, the Cu(I)-chloride field would broaden and stretch toward higher pH (T-strengthened chlorocuprates are well known in hydrothermal geochemistry), and citrate would begin to decarboxylate — so use the pattern above as the qualitative baseline, not the numerical answer.
