01Approach

The solver treats every complex as forming from the aquo ion plus its listed ligand set with a single stability constant β. Ligand acidity ladders set αCit3−(pH), αGly−(pH) and αNH3(pH). Because CT,Cu + CT,Fe = 2 mM sits far below the ligand totals (10 – 100 mM), free ligand activities are taken as CT,L · αL,deprot(pH), without depletion by metal binding — worst-case error < 15%.

At each pH the solver computes (i) the free-ion mole fractions αMn+ from a direct sum over all complexes, (ii) the conditional standard potentials E′°(pH) of every redox pair after complexation, (iii) the maximum dissolved metal that can coexist with each hydroxide/oxide solid, and (iv) whether Cu(I) disproportionates. A phase is drawn where its region is the largest single form of that metal in a mass-balanced (pH, E) point.

Cu partitioning summary

Cu(II) never stays as Cu2+ above pH ≈ 3.5. Between pH 4 and 8 it is progressively locked into Cu-citrate — first CuHCit(aq) then CuCit−, settling as Cu(Cit)24−. From pH ≈ 8 to 11.4, glycine (αGly− rising) wins with Cu(Gly)2(aq); ammonia forms Cu(NH3)42+ in the same window but the 15.03 log β of Cu(Gly)2 keeps it slightly under glycine. Only above pH ≈ 11.4 does Cu(OH)2(s) precipitate.

Cu(I) is a chloride complex CuCl2− for pH 0 – 4, then disproportionates through pH ≈ 5 – 7 (no dissolved Cu(I) window), and reopens as Cu(NH3)2+ around pH 7 – 11.5. Cu2O(s) forms only in a narrow wedge above pH 11.5, immediately under Cu(OH)2(s).

Fe partitioning summary

Fe(III) is Fe3+/FeCl2+ only in a sliver at pH 0 – 1; from pH 1 upward it is essentially all citrate: FeHCit+ → FeCit(aq) → Fe(Cit)23−, then hydroxo-citrate mixed species. Only at pH ≈ 7.5 does the amorphous Fe(OH)3(s) — upshifted by more than four pH units from a citrate-free diagram — finally saturate.

Fe(II) stays largely aqueous: Fe2+ to about pH 3.7, then FeHCit / FeCit− up to pH 9; Fe(Gly)2 in a small window around pH 9 – 9.4; Fe(OH)2(s) above ≈ 9.4. The Fe(III)/Fe(II) boundary slides from +0.73 V at pH 0 to below −0.6 V at pH 14, driven by preferential Fe(III)–citrate stabilisation.

08Implications for co-electrodeposition

The Fe(III)/Fe(II) potential falls below Cu deposition across almost the entire mid-pH range.

At pH 0 in the aquo picture, Fe(III)/Fe(II) sits at +0.73 V and Cu(II)/Cu(0) at +0.34 V — Fe(III) is a strong oxidiser of Cu(0). In the citrate/glycine/NH3 electrolyte, by pH ≈ 4 the Fe(III)/Fe(II) line is already at +0.24 V and Cu(II)/Cu(0) is at +0.19 V; by pH 8 the Fe(III)/Fe(II) potential is negative and the aqueous Cu(II)/Cu(0) transition is near 0 V. Citrate has effectively inverted the Cu-vs-Fe corrosion picture.

There is no (pH, E) window where dissolved Cu(II) and dissolved Fe(0) or Fe(II)-only can coexist without Fe(OH)3(s).

Fe(OH)3(s, am) starts to form at pH ≈ 7.5, so any Cu-deposition bath with pH above ~7 already carries an Fe(OH)3 phase unless Fe(III) is continuously reduced. For a clean Cu bath, either (i) work at pH < 7 where Fe is speciated as Fe(Cit)23−/Fe(II)-citrate, or (ii) work below the Fe(III)/Fe(II) boundary so all Fe is Fe(II)-citrate and Fe(OH)3 cannot form.

Co-deposition of Cu and Fe requires reaching the Fe(II)/Fe(0) line at ≈ −0.55 to −0.85 V.

The Fe(II)/Fe(0) line is pinned near −0.55 V at low pH and drops with increasing pH (to about −0.83 V under Fe(OH)2(s) above pH 9.4). Cu deposition, meanwhile, becomes possible at much less negative E — as high as +0.13 V for Cu(I) → Cu(s) at pH 0, or ≈ −0.2 V for Cu(OH)2/Cu(NH3)2+ → Cu(s) at pH 11. The ~0.4 – 0.9 V gap between Cu-deposition and Fe-deposition potentials is almost entirely due to citrate-glycine-ammonia complexation of Cu(II) — a bare-aquo diagram would place Cu deposition 0.34 V positive of Fe and the two would never co-deposit at reasonable current densities.

Cu(I) is a real speciated intermediate here, not a bookkeeping artefact.

In pure water Cu(I) disproportionates across almost the whole pH range. In this electrolyte, chloride opens a CuCl2−/CuCl32− window at pH 0 – 4, and ammonia opens a Cu(NH3)2+ window at pH 7 – 11.5. Any Cu deposition mechanism going through a one-electron intermediate is chemically supported in these bands.
