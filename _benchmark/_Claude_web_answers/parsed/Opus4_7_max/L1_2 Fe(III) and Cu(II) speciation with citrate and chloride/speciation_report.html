Fe(III) & Cu(II) in a citrate + chloride pot

How two competing metals share 5 mM citrate and 0.1 M chloride from pH 2 to 10, and what the Nernst equation makes of both couples.

Fe (total)1.0 mM
Cu (total)1.0 mM
citrate5.0 mM
chloride0.10 M
T25 °C
I0.1 m

§1Method & solver

At each grid point (pH, E) the solver enforces mass balance on every dissolved element and Nernst equilibrium on the two redox couples. Chloride is held at 0.10 M throughout — the metals take at most ~4 mM of Cl, so the free-Cl approximation is safe to under 4%. The unknowns reduce to a single scalar, [Cit3−]free, and the citrate mass-balance residual is solved by Brent's method between log[Cit3−] = −25 and −0.5.

FeT = [Fe3+] · αFe(III) + [Fe2+] · αFe(II)
CuT = [Cu2+] · αCu(II) + [Cu+] · αCu(I)
[Fe3+] / [Fe2+] = 10(E − 0.771) / 0.05916
[Cu2+] / [Cu+] = 10(E − 0.153) / 0.05916

Each α is the side-reaction coefficient — the sum over hydrolysis, chloro-, and citrato- complexes weighted by the current [OH−], [Cl−], [Cit3−], and [HCit2−]. After the aqueous solve, three solids are tested for saturation and, if oversaturated, cap the corresponding free-ion activity: 2-line ferrihydrite (Fe(OH)3(s)), amorphous Cu(OH)2(s), and nantokite (CuCl(s)). Cu(s) forms when [Cu+] exceeds 10(E − 0.521)/0.05916.

Every equilibrium constant used is tabulated in §2. The database follows the critical compilations (NIST 46, Baes & Mesmer's Hydrolysis of Cations, and Martell & Smith's Critical Stability Constants); polynuclear species that are minor at 1 mM metal and I = 0.1 M (the Fe2(OH)24+ dimer and the various Cu–Cit dinuclear pairs) are omitted.

§3The joint E–pH picture

Two Pourbaix maps, one per metal, drawn on the same pot: identical chloride, identical citrate, identical solved [Cit3−] at every grid point. Whatever citrate one metal grabs, the other doesn't get. Dashed lines are the H2O stability window.

Why the two metals partition so differently. Both Fe(III) and Cu(II) form Cit–M complexes stronger than either forms with Cl−. But Fe(III) is so hard a Lewis acid that ferrihydrite outcompetes citrate above pH 5.5, whereas Cu(II) — a softer, borderline acid — keeps its citrate complex intact up to pH 8 before Cu(OH)2(s) starts to matter. Chloride only matters for the reduced forms: it hardly touches Fe(II), but it dominates Cu(I) speciation entirely.

§4Species distributions at fixed E

Oxic (E = +0.60 V vs SHE)

An air-equilibrated water sits near this potential once biology or organic matter has bled off a bit of the O2/H2O headroom. Under these conditions Fe is trapped as Fe(III), Cu as Cu(II) — the two metals compete for the same citrate pool.

Mid (E = +0.15 V vs SHE)

This slice cuts right through the Fe(III)/Fe(II) crossover at circum-neutral pH and lies just below the Cu(I)/Cu(II) crossover. The two metals behave differently: Fe still ends up as ferrihydrite above pH 6 because Fe(III) is thermodynamically favoured there; Cu is stabilised as Cu(I)–chloride until citrate can bind Cu(II) strongly enough (~pH 6) to pull the couple over.

§7Reading the diagram: what actually wins where

pH 2–3, oxic. Fe(III) is quantitatively FeHCit+ — the reaction Fe3+ + HCit2− ⇄ FeHCit+ with log K = 12.3 easily beats the strongest FeCln combination (log β2Fe(III) · [Cl−]2 = 2.13 − 2 = 0.13 in log units) at 5 mM citrate. Cu(II) is mostly free Cu2+ with ~20% as CuCl+; citrate is too protonated to compete.

pH 4–5. Citrate has slid past its second pKa; CuCit− becomes dominant (log K = 5.90 acting on the growing pool of L3−). Fe(III) is still on FeHCit+. This is the only pH range where both metals actively share the citrate: Fe holds 20%, Cu takes another 20% — and 60% of the citrate is still free/protonated. There is no thermodynamic competition here: 5 mM citrate is plenty for 2 mM total metal, even at a 1:1 stoichiometry.

pH 5.5–6. Ferrihydrite crashes out at these citrate levels — the mixed FeCit(OH)− pathway is not strong enough to keep pace with Fe(OH)3(s). Fe abandons the citrate pool entirely. Cu doubles down: with FeCit+ gone, [L3−] rises, and Cu shifts from CuCit− toward CuCit24− (log β2 = 8.50).

pH 7–8. Cu is roughly half CuCit−, half CuCit24−. Then near pH 8 the Cu(OH)2(s) line crosses and copper follows iron into the solid. The freed citrate simply stays as L3−.

pH 9–10. Both metals are essentially quantitative solids. Any residual soluble Fe is Fe(OH)4− (~10−9 M), any residual Cu is CuCit24− at similarly trace levels.

Reducing side (E < 0.2 V). Iron drops to Fe(II), where citrate binding is 7 orders of magnitude weaker (log K = 4.4 vs 11.4). Fe(II) still binds citrate above pH 5 because chloride is even weaker, but the driving force is small. Copper's behaviour is entirely different: below the Cu(s) line (~0 to +0.1 V, moving with pH) the metal plates out; between +0.1 and ~+0.4 V, CuCl2− owns the solution because the chloride stabilisation of Cu(I) is enormous. The Cu(II)/Cu(I) effective potential in 0.1 M Cl− and 5 mM citrate at pH 5 is roughly:

E' = 0.153 − 0.05916 · log{ αCu(I)(0.1 M Cl) / αCu(II)([L3−]) }
≈ 0.153 − 0.05916 · log{ 103.5 / (102.5) } ≈ +0.09 V

i.e. citrate binding of Cu(II) partly cancels the chloride stabilisation of Cu(I), and the couple ends up an order of magnitude closer to zero than its bare value.

§8Caveats

  • Ferrihydrite solubility is soft. log *Ks = 3.5 is a fresh 2-line ferrihydrite. Aged goethite is 10–100× less soluble, which would push the Fe(OH)3(s) boundary down to pH ~4 and eliminate the Fe(III)–citrate region on the oxic side. If your pot is minutes old, use 3.5; if it has aged for days, use 1.5 and expect the diagram to redraw.
  • Polynuclear Fe(III)–citrate. At 1 mM Fe and pH 5–7, Fe2Cit2(OH)22− and related trimers actually stabilise Fe(III) in solution well beyond what the mononuclear model predicts. They're omitted here for clarity, but they push the ferrihydrite line up ~1 pH unit if you include them.
  • Cu(II)–citrate polynuclear. The Cu2Cit22− dimer is real at millimolar Cu but again small; including it broadens the CuCit domain by a few tenths of a pH unit.
  • [Cl−] held constant. Total Cl− is 100 mM and no metal takes more than ~4 mM, so the free-Cl approximation is at worst 4%.
  • Ionic strength. Constants are cited at I = 0.1 M where possible. Corrections to and from I = 0 use Davies where extrapolation was needed. Systematic error on any log K is ~± 0.2 units.
  • No CO2. Real air-equilibrated solutions carry ~10−5 M carbonate at pH 8, which would compete for Cu (CuCO3) and slightly for Fe (siderite is not stable at these E's). Add carbonate explicitly if the pot is open to air.
