2. Conditional side reaction coefficients αM(z)

α is the ratio [total dissolved M in oxidation state z] / [free Mz+] at the given pH with the fixed ligand pool. Because αCu(II) ≫ αCu(I), the Cu(II)/Cu(I) couple shifts more negative when Cu(II) is complexed more strongly than Cu(I) — exactly the opposite of what would be favourable for stabilising Cu(I) in an ammoniacal bath, and it is why Cu(I) never appears as a major species above pH ≈ 8 despite the strong Cu(NH₃)₂⁺.

3. Principal region boundaries and junctions

Cu partitioning

Vertical pH-only boundaries in the dissolved Cu(II) region (E-independent) occur where two complexes swap dominance. Horizontal / diagonal boundaries between oxidation states follow the conditional Nernst potentials in §2.

  • pH 0 – 3.25: Cu²⁺ is the dominant dissolved Cu(II) form (chloride complexes never rise above ≈20 %).
  • pH 3.25 – 4.25: switches to Cu(gly)⁺. Boundary set by log β₁(Gly) + log αGly⁻(pH) = 0 → pH ≈ 3.25.
  • pH 4.25 – 5.35: transient CuCit⁻ window (citrate becomes fully deprotonated near pH 6.4; below the second glycine deprotonation, citrate wins).
  • pH 5.35 – 11.65: Cu(gly)₂ dominates a very wide field (log β₂ = 15.6; two Gly⁻ arms make it the strongest 2:1 complex here).
  • pH 11.65 – 13.90: Cu(OH)₂(s) precipitates because [Cu²⁺]free demanded by Cu(gly)₂ falls below the Ksp threshold. Solver junction: pH 11.65 (Cu(gly)₂ · Cu(OH)₂), pH 13.90 (redissolution to Cu(OH)₄²⁻).
  • Cu(I) speciation: CuCl₂⁻ below pH 6.7, Cu(NH₃)₂⁺ above. The field only spans −0.03 V ≲ E ≲ +0.12 V and shrinks continuously with pH; above pH ≈ 7 the Cu(II)/Cu(I) formal potential drops below the Cu(I)/Cu(0) formal potential and Cu(I) is thermodynamically eliminated by disproportionation.
  • Cu₂O(s) island: opens near pH ≈ 10.6 up to pH 13.5 at E ≈ −0.15 to 0 V. Cu(NH₃)₂⁺ is strong (log β₂ = 10.9) but insufficient at high pH to keep [Cu⁺]free above the Cu₂O solubility limit (log[Cu⁺]max = −0.84 − pH − ½ log α).
  • Cu(s) upper boundary (ECu(II)/Cu): starts at +0.245 V at pH 0, turns negative around pH ≈ 8 owing to strong Cu(II) complexation, reaches −0.083 V at pH 10 and levels near −0.10 V at high pH; H₂O/H₂ line sits far below it, so Cu will plate over the entire pH range at E ≤ that curve.

Fe partitioning

  • pH 0 – 1.25: FeCl²⁺ dominates (Cl⁻ is the strongest ligand available before Cit³⁻ deprotonates). Fe³⁺ is a minority (αFe(III)≈5–6).
  • pH 1.25 – 4.60: FeCit(aq) (log β = 13.1). Citrate wins as soon as even 1 % of it is in the Cit³⁻ form (~pH 1.5).
  • pH 4.60 – 6.10: Fe(OH)Cit⁻ takes over (log β for Fe³⁺+Cit³⁻+OH⁻ = 22.3, so the switch is at pH such that log βOHcit + log[OH⁻] = log βCit, i.e. pH = pKw + (13.1 − 22.3) = 4.58).
  • pH 6.10 – 10.60: Fe(OH)₂Cit²⁻; same argument applied twice.
  • pH ≳ 7.0 (Fe(III), above the FeCit line): Fe(OH)₃(s,am) starts to precipitate because even the very strong citrate complex cannot hold 1 mM Fe(III) against the amorphous ferric hydroxide Ksp. Solver junction: pH 6.95; Fe(OH)₃ dominates almost the entire high-E, high-pH quadrant.
  • Fe(II) speciation: Fe²⁺ (pH ≤ 4.5) → FeCit⁻ (4.5–9.25) → Fe(gly)₂ (9.25–11.7).
  • Fe(OH)₂(s) opens at pH ≈ 9.4 for Fe(II) at 1 mM; the Fe(II)–glycine complex is not strong enough (log β₂ = 7.7) to keep it dissolved.
  • Fe(III)/Fe(II) formal potential: shifted from +0.771 V to +0.056 V at pH 7 — a 715 mV cathodic shift due to the ~10¹²× stronger Fe(III) complexation by citrate compared with Fe(II). Above pH ≈ 8 it drops below H₂O/H₂, so every Fe(III) present will be autoreduced by any mild reducer in an ammoniacal-citrate bath.
  • Fe(s) upper boundary: sits at ≈ −0.54 V at low pH, drifts to −0.85 V at pH 14; almost coincident with H₂O/H₂ from pH ≈ 10 on, so Fe deposition is inseparable from parasitic H₂ evolution above pH ≈ 10.

Key observations for the electrolyte designer:

  • Even with all four ligands acting on Cu(II) (chloride, ammonia, glycine, citrate), the smallest Cu–Fe deposition gap is about ~550 mV at pH 10. That is small enough for iron-rich Fe–Cu alloys to be deposited from a single bath, but Fe deposition sits right at the H₂O/H₂ line, so current efficiency will be limited by co-evolving H₂.
  • The commonly used tactic — ammine complexation to depress the Cu potential — is only partially effective here. Above pH ≈ 8 the four Cu(gly)₂ arms overwhelm the four Cu(NH₃)₄²⁺ arms because the free NH₃ activity is limited by the NH₄⁺/NH₃ equilibrium (αNH₃(pH 8) ≈ 0.05), while Gly⁻ is nearly fully deprotonated already at pH ≈ 8.5.
  • Fe(III) is very effectively sequestered by citrate: over pH 2 – 6.9 no Fe(OH)₃ forms and Fe(III) sits entirely as FeCit / Fe(OH)Cit⁻. Above pH ≈ 7 the Fe(OH)₃(s,am) field opens; the user's exclusion of Fe₂O₃ / FeOOH / Fe₃O₄ simply keeps that region labelled as the amorphous hydroxide rather than a crystalline oxide.
  • Cu(I) is only observable as an intermediate in a narrow band around E ≈ +0.1 V, and only below pH 8 where CuCl₂⁻ or Cu(NH₃)₂⁺ is strong enough to survive disproportionation. Baths intended to plate through Cu(I) intermediates should therefore be run below pH 6.5 with elevated chloride, or between pH 8 – 10 with elevated free ammonia, and away from the Cu₂O island.

5. Caveats

Approximations kept explicit. (i) Ligand concentrations were treated as analytical × acid-base α, i.e. free-ligand depletion by metal binding was neglected (≤10 % effect at 1 mM metal, 10 mM ligand). (ii) Only mononuclear complexes reported by Smith & Martell / NIST 46 were included; polynuclear hydroxides of Cu and Fe are known to matter only above ~10⁻³ M free metal — that threshold is not reached anywhere on this diagram. (iii) Anhydrous oxides (Fe₂O₃, CuO, FeOOH, Fe₃O₄) were removed per request; their omission is thermodynamically consistent only under fast, water-rich deposition kinetics where the amorphous hydroxide precipitates first and does not dehydrate. (iv) Activity coefficients were not re-applied to the tabulated log β; the values quoted are conditional constants at I ≈ 0.1 m (most already reported at that ionic strength; those originally at I = 0 were Davies-corrected before being written into the table). All conditional potentials reported are direct, unconditional Nernst expressions using those log β.
